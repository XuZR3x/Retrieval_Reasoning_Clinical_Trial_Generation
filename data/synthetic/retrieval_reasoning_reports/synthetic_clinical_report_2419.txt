The clinical trial was aimed at addressing the unmet need for effective treatments in patients with advanced solid tumors characterized by elevated ISG15 expression. Unfortunately, despite its intent to explore the therapeutic potential of irinotecan, the trial faced critical setbacks that ultimately led to its termination.

Fundamentally, this trial was impeded by inadequate patient enrollment, whereby a mere 10 subjects were enrolled over a span exceeding two years. This shortfall significantly compromised the trial's statistical power, rendering it inadequate to draw definitive conclusions about the safety or efficacy of irinotecan in the target patient population.

Furthermore, the rigidity in the selection criteria regarding the inclusion of patients with high ISG15 expression may have inadvertently limited the population's heterogeneity, excluding potentially responsive patients with varying tumor profiles. This lack of biomarker diversity might have contributed to the inability to adequately stratify individuals who could have benefited from the treatment.

The ambitious primary outcome of demonstrating a substantial objective response rate (ORR) within a cohort largely constituted by heavily pre-treated patients presented a therapeutic mismatch. Patients enrolled often exhibited a complex history of resistance to multiple prior regimens, thus impairing the likelihood of achieving the anticipated clinical responses with single-agent irinotecan therapy.

Moreover, well-documented adverse effects associated with irinotecan, such as severe neutropenia and gastrointestinal toxicities, potentially deterred patient participation and retention during the trial's course. These safety concerns may have compounded the difficulties in achieving the necessary sample size for meaningful analysis.

In essence, this clinical trial elucidates several crucial lessons regarding the challenges of designing studies centered on irinotecan for advanced solid tumors, underscoring the necessity of multifaceted recruitment strategies and the careful consideration of patient-centered endpoints that align with real-world clinical challenges faced in oncology.
