**Failed Clinical Trial of Insulin: Enhanced Insulin Dosing Protocol during Hyperglycemia in Pediatric Populations with Type 1 Diabetes**

**A Monocentric, Randomized, Controlled Trial to Evaluate the Effectiveness of an Enhanced Insulin Dosing Protocol during Acute Hyperglycemia in Pediatric Patients with Type 1 Diabetes in the Emergency Department**

The purpose of this study is to investigate whether an enhanced insulin dosing protocol, which incorporates an increased basal insulin infusion rate and supplemental bolus doses, can achieve faster glycemic control in children presenting with acute hyperglycemia compared to standard insulin therapy. This study aimed to assess the potential of this enhanced protocol to minimize hospital stay and mitigate complications related to hyperglycemia.

Approximately 80 participants were enrolled and randomized to either control (standard insulin protocol) or intervention (enhanced dosing protocol). The intervention group received a customized insulin infusion based on the severity of hyperglycemia and concurrent carbohydrate ingestion during emergency department visits. Blood glucose levels were monitored at intervals, accompanied by structured assessments of clinical outcomes.

Inclusion Criteria:
- Age 6-18 years
- Clinical diagnosis of type 1 diabetes for more than one year
- Hospital admission for acute hyperglycemia (blood glucose > 240 mg/dL) 
- Ability to adhere to the treatment protocol and provide informed consent/assent

Exclusion Criteria:
- Comorbid significant medical conditions that could interfere with insulin administration (e.g., renal failure, severe liver dysfunction)
- Severe psychiatric disorders impacting the ability to comply with the study protocol
- History of recurrent hypoglycemia (BG < 70 mg/dL) within the past six months
- Presence of acute metabolic disturbances (e.g., ketoacidosis) upon presentation

Results indicated a substantial incidence of hypoglycemic events in the intervention group, with more than 30% experiencing clinical symptoms requiring intervention, compared to 10% in the control group. Furthermore, frequent fluctuations in glucose levels stressed the importance of individualized treatment strategies.

Challenges faced in this trial included:

1. **Inadequate Management of Metabolic Variability**: The unique metabolic responses of pediatric patients under acute stress were not effectively captured, causing over-corrections in dosages that led to an increased rate of hypoglycemia.

2. **Suboptimal Monitoring Parameters**: The trial failed to incorporate comprehensive continuous glucose monitoring systems (CGMS), resulting in inadequate tracking of rapid glucose fluctuations during critical treatment phases, which adversely affected insulin titration accuracy.

3. **Participant Selection Bias**: The study enrolled participants exhibiting wide-ranging pre-existing metabolic control, complicating aggregation of outcomes and skewing efficacy assessments of the intervention.

4. **Psychosocial Factors Affecting Compliance**: The dynamics of patient and caregiver adherence to the intervention protocol were not fully accounted for, leading to inconsistencies in insulin administration timing and dosing accuracy.

Ultimately, the enhanced dosing protocol did not demonstrate statistically significant improvements in time to achieve target blood glucose levels or reductions in length of stay, leading to termination of the trial based on predefined efficacy endpoints.

**Primary Endpoint**: Time to achieve blood glucose levels between 80-140 mg/dL.

**Secondary Endpoint**: Length of hospital stay and incidence of hypoglycemic events (<70 mg/dL).

This trial underscores the necessity of precise dosing protocols, robust monitoring methodologies, and an awareness of the inherent metabolic variability within pediatric populations to successfully evaluate insulin interventions in acute care settings.
