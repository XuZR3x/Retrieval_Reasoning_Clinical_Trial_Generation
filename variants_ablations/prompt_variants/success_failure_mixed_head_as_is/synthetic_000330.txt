**Failed Clinical Trial of Olanzapine: Efficacy of Olanzapine Versus Standard Care for Nausea and Vomiting in Patients With Advanced Stage Liver Disease**

**A Multi-Center Randomized Controlled Trial Evaluating the Efficacy of Olanzapine Compared to Standard Supportive Care for Nausea and Vomiting in Patients With Advanced Stage Liver Disease**

**Principal Objective:**  
This study aims to evaluate the effect of olanzapine on the frequency and severity of nausea and vomiting in patients with advanced liver disease undergoing supportive care, compared to a control group receiving standard antiemetic therapy.

**Inclusion Criteria:**  
- Adults aged 18 years and older.  
- Diagnosed with advanced liver disease classified as Child-Pugh Class B or C.  
- Experiencing moderate to severe nausea as defined by a visual analog scale (VAS) score ≥ 30 mm.  
- No prior treatment with investigational drugs or olanzapine within the last 30 days.  
- Patients who provide informed consent and have adequate cognitive function to participate.

**Exclusion Criteria:**  
- Pregnant or breastfeeding women.  
- Patients with psychiatric disorders or a history of substance abuse.  
- Recent surgical interventions or those with active gastrointestinal obstruction.  
- Patients currently receiving chemotherapeutic agents or other strong antiemetics (including but not limited to 5-HT3 receptor antagonists and metoclopramide).

**Methodology:**  
This study was designed as a double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group trial. Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either olanzapine (10 mg daily) or standard supportive care involving dexamethasone and ondansetron. The primary endpoint was measured by the frequency of episodes of nausea and vomiting over a 14-day period, evaluated using the VAS and patient diaries. Secondary endpoints included assessment of overall quality of life through standardized questionnaires.

**Contextual Considerations:**  
This trial faced several critical challenges leading to its discontinuation. A significant variability within the patient population concerning the severity of liver disease and concurrent conditions significantly confounded the results, as noted in previous trials of olanzapine for antiemetic efficacy.  

Additionally, stringent eligibility criteria substantially limited participant recruitment, resulting in a smaller-than-anticipated sample size that diminished the study's statistical power and generalizability. As a result, insufficient external validity was observed, undermining the clinical applicability of findings to diverse populations.

Moreover, the double-blind design introduced variability in subjective outcomes given the psychoactive nature of olanzapine, which may have led to observer bias, complicating the assessment of trial endpoints such as nausea and vomiting frequency. Preliminary analyses indicated that adverse events, particularly sedation, led to poor adherence among participants receiving olanzapine, further complicating the therapeutic efficacy evaluation and subsequent analysis.

Finally, concerns arose regarding the pharmacological action of olanzapine as an antipsychotic rather than an antiemetic, which raised questions on its appropriateness for managing nausea and vomiting stemming from liver disease, ultimately leading to the study's premature termination.

**Conclusions:**  
This clinical trial exemplifies substantial challenges in investigating olanzapine's efficacy for nausea and vomiting in advanced liver disease, underscoring the need for more tailored investigational approaches and consideration of the pharmacological profiles specific to the pathophysiological mechanisms at play. Further investigations must reassess olanzapine's viability within this context while evaluating patient characteristics that could mitigate variability in clinical outcomes.
