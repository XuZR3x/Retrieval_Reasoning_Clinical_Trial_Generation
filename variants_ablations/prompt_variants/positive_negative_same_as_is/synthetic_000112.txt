Negative clinical trial of raltegravir: Raltegravir Efficacy in Relapsing-Remitting Multiple Sclerosis: A Phase II Randomized Controlled Trial

**Background**

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is characterized by an autoimmune-mediated attack on the central nervous system, resulting in demyelination and neurodegeneration. Antiretroviral therapies (ART) have been proposed as potential modulators of immune dysregulation in MS, particularly through the modulation of human endogenous retroviruses (HERVs). Raltegravir, an integrase strand transfer inhibitor, has demonstrated promise in reducing HERV expression; however, the complex interplay of MS pathophysiology requires rigorous investigation.

**Study Design**

This multicenter, double-blind, randomized controlled trial aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of raltegravir in patients diagnosed with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS). Patients were randomly assigned to receive either raltegravir or placebo for six months. Primary outcomes were assessed through changes in gadolinium-enhanced MRI lesions and clinical relapses.

**Methods**

- **Participants**: Adults aged 18-50 with a confirmed diagnosis of RRMS per the McDonald criteria, with an EDSS score of 0-5.0, and evidence of at least one relapse in the previous 12 months.
  
- **Intervention**: Participants received 400 mg of raltegravir orally twice daily or matching placebo, continued for a total of 6 months.
  
- **Assessment**: Baseline gadolinium-enhanced MRI was performed, and monthly MRIs were conducted throughout the intervention period. Neurological assessments were conducted monthly along with blood sampling for biomarkers of inflammation and viral load.

**Endpoints**

The primary endpoint was the number of new or recurrent gadolinium-enhancing lesions on MRI compared to baseline, and the secondary endpoints involved clinical relapse rates and changes in inflammatory biomarkers.

**Inclusion Criteria**

- Aged 18-50 years
- Diagnosis of RRMS according to the revised McDonald Criteria
- EDSS score of 0-5.0
- History of at least one relapse in the past 12 months

**Exclusion Criteria**

- Prior or current use of immunomodulatory or immunosuppressive therapy within 6 months
- Active human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection
- Pregnancy or lactation
- Any serious comorbidities that could affect study outcomes, including autoimmune disorders

**Results**

The study enrolled 200 participants, with 100 in the raltegravir group and 100 in the placebo group. At the conclusion of the 6-month treatment period, the primary outcome measure revealed no significant difference in the number of new or recurrent gadolinium-enhancing lesions between the raltegravir and placebo groups (Median difference: 0.5 lesions, p=0.45). Moreover, clinical relapse rates did not differ significantly (Raltegravir group: 12% vs. Placebo group: 14%, p=0.62).

Secondary analyses indicated no variation in inflammatory biomarker levels between groups, challenging the hypothesized immunomodulatory effects of raltegravir.

**Discussion**

The trial results indicate a failure to demonstrate clinically meaningful efficacy of raltegravir in RRMS, attributed to several factors:

1. **Inadequate Mechanistic Justification**: The biological rationale for targeting HERVs with an integrase inhibitor lacked robust preclinical validation concerning MS.

2. **Study Design Limitations**: Six months of observation may have been insufficient to capture significant disease-modifying effects in the unpredictable course of RRMS.

3. **Patient Variability**: The heterogeneous patient population, with diverse comorbidities and disease histories, potentially diluted treatment effects.

4. **Imprecision of Neuroimaging as a Biomarker**: Gadolinium-enhanced MRI may not adequately reflect subtle changes in disease activity or treatment response, leading to misinterpretation of efficacy.

5. **Underlying Disease Complexity**: Active virological and immunological pathways in MS patients may not be addressed by a single-agent therapy directed at retroviral elements.

**Conclusion**

This Phase II trial underscores the need for more targeted hypotheses in exploring antiviral treatments for complex autoimmune disorders such as multiple sclerosis. As a result, further investigation into the role of HERVs and their modulation in MS pathogenesis remains necessary, particularly in light of limitations highlighted by this intervention.
