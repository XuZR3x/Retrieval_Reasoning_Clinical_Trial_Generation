**Failed clinical trial of ezetimibe: Ezetimibe in Patients with Advanced Chronic Kidney Disease and Dyslipidemia**

**Ezetimibe in Patients with Advanced Chronic Kidney Disease and Dyslipidemia: A Randomized Controlled Trial**

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 10% of the global population, and dyslipidemia is common among patients with CKD, contributing to the increased cardiovascular risk observed in this population. Dyslipidemia in CKD is characterized by elevated levels of triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), alongside a reduction in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Current therapeutic management options often hinge on dietary modifications and statin therapy; however, many CKD patients either do not achieve the desired lipid profile or experience statin-associated adverse events. Ezetimibe, a selective cholesterol absorption inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy in lowering LDL-C levels but its effects in patients with CKD remain poorly understood.

This study was designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ezetimibe, administered at a dose of 10 mg once daily for 24 weeks, compared to placebo in patients with advanced CKD (stages 4 and 5) and dyslipidemia. The primary aim was to determine whether treatment with ezetimibe would lead to a significant reduction in LDL-C levels from baseline, which could potentially mitigate the cardiovascular risk in this vulnerable cohort. The study sought to enroll 100 participants aged 18 years and older, each with documented dyslipidemia defined as an LDL-C of >130 mg/dL, and a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of <30 mL/min.

Inclusion Criteria:
- Adults aged 18 years or older.
- Diagnosis of advanced CKD (stages 4 and 5) as determined by estimated GFR <30 mL/min.
- Dyslipidemia defined as LDL-C >130 mg/dL.
- Signed informed consent from participants.
  
Exclusion Criteria:
- Active liver disease (defined as ALT or AST >3 times the upper limit of normal).
- Significant musculoskeletal disorders or other contraindications to drug treatment (e.g., myopathy).
- History of hypersensitivity reactions to ezetimibe or related compounds.
- Concurrent use of medications that may significantly alter lipid metabolism.

The trial's primary endpoint was the percentage change in LDL-C from baseline to 24 weeks. Secondary endpoints included changes in triglycerides, HDL-C, and overall safety profiles assessed through biochemistry and patient-reported outcomes. 

The authors hypothesized that ezetimibe would successfully reduce LDL-C and improve the lipid profile without significant adverse effects, especially given the absence of major lipid-modifying agents that are both effective and tolerable in this patient population.

However, the trial faced multiple challenges throughout its course. The anticipated recruitment rate was not met largely due to strict eligibility criteria, leading to an insufficient sample size of only 42 participants. Moreover, notable side effects were observed in a subset of patients, including muscular pain and gastrointestinal disturbances. In terms of efficacy, results indicated only a modest and non-significant reduction in LDL-C levels compared to placebo (-4.2% vs. -3.5%, p=0.67). Furthermore, secondary outcomes also did not demonstrate statistically significant differences, which included unchanged triglyceride levels. 

In conclusion, this trial failed to achieve its primary endpoint and did not present enough evidence to support the use of ezetimibe as an effective treatment for dyslipidemia in patients with advanced CKD. The results underscore the need for further research into lipid management strategies in this population, perhaps focusing on alternative pharmacotherapies or targeting other metabolic pathways. The findings will inform future clinical trial designs and treatment guidelines for dyslipidemic patients with advanced renal impairment.
