Failed clinical trial of rosiglitazone: Rosiglitazone Efficacy in Reducing Diabetic Nephropathy Progression in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus and Microalbuminuria

Rosiglitazone Efficacy in Reducing Diabetic Nephropathy Progression in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus and Microalbuminuria

This study aimed to determine the efficacy of rosiglitazone in mitigating the progression of diabetic nephropathy, as measured by changes in urinary albumin excretion rates (UAER) over a 24-month period among patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and microalbuminuria. Enrollment challenges and emerging safety concerns regarding rosiglitazone led to the termination of the study on 18-Aug-10.

Inclusion Criteria:
- Male or female patients aged 30-75 years with a confirmed diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria.
- Participants exhibiting microalbuminuria, defined as UAER of 30-300 mg/g creatinine, on two separate occasions within the screening period.
- Participants must have a HbA1c between 6.5% and 9.5% at screening, indicating inadequate glycemic control.
- Patients must provide documented consent and be willing to comply with study protocol during the intervention period.

Exclusion Criteria:
- Patients with established chronic kidney disease (CKD) with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min at baseline.
- Patients with a history of cardiovascular disease (CVD) within the past 12 months, including myocardial infarction or stroke.
- Patients concurrently using other glucose-lowering agents or undergoing treatment with nephrotoxic medications.
- Individuals with significant hepatic dysfunction, defined as ALT or AST levels exceeding 2.5 times the upper limit of normal.
- Patients with an existing history of severe heart failure or those on treatment for NYHA class III or IV heart failure will be excluded.

The primary endpoint of this investigation was the alteration in UAER, assessed at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months. Secondary endpoints included changes in eGFR, quality of life as measured by the Diabetes Quality of Life (DQOL) scale, and incidence of adverse events, particularly cardiovascular events and renal-related complications.

Despite initial enthusiasm regarding the potential renal protective effects of rosiglitazone, the trial faced difficulty in recruitment. A substantial number of potential participants were excluded due to pre-existing CVD and renal impairment, conditions likely prevalent within the study population. Additionally, emerging safety concerns surrounding rosiglitazone's cardiovascular profile contributed to hesitancy among potential enrolled subjects, leading ultimately to insufficient enrollment to meet study power and objectives.

In conclusion, while this investigational study sought to clarify the role of rosiglitazone in managing diabetic nephropathy progression, multiple factors including restrictive eligibility criteria and rising safety apprehensions culminated in the trial's failure. Future research endeavors might explore alternative therapeutic agents or stricter management protocols to increase participant safety and broaden eligibility, aiming to investigate the intricate relationship between glycemic control and renal outcomes in the diabetic population.
